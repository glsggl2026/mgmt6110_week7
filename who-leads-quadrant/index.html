My agent card | Draft reply to ask to reschedule on my Google schedule | finished 17 Oct 2026, 12:00 PM SGT

  1. The job: When someone emails my Gmail account to make an appointment, or sends me a calendar invite, with the keyword “appointment” in the subject, it drafts a short, polite reply that greets them by first name and responds to what they asked. If they want to meet in the future, it invites them to book a time on my shared Google schedule; if the request isn’t realistic, it replies politely. It works for me, a postgrad student who juggles postgrad timelines, personal life and project meetings, so senders can book directly and I spend less time on rescheduling.
  2. The trigger: A new email or a new calendar invite in my Gmail Inbox with “appointment” in the subject (Gmail – New Email Matching Search: in:inbox subject:appointment), checked about every 15 minutes.
  3. The tools: Gmail – New Email Matching Search (reads the request); AI by Zapier (writes the reply wording, no tools attached); Gmail – Create Draft Reply (saves the draft in the same conversation, To: the sender, with my booking link and sign-off as fixed text).
  4. It must never: Send an email without my review. The setting that stops it: the Zap’s only Gmail action is Create Draft Reply. It has no Send Email or Reply action, so it cannot send.
  5. Where a person checks: I (the account owner) prepare my data, then check each draft in two ways.
    • Preparing my data: because the Zap only catches emails with “appointment” in the subject and cannot read my calendar, I screen other scheduling emails myself, set my priorities and block “must-do” events in my calendar, so my shared Google schedule stays realistic and people book against my latest availability.
    • Check 1, accuracy: before sending, I open each draft in Gmail and check that the greeting uses the right name, the reply invents no dates or times and does not accept a date that has already passed, the booking link opens, and the draft sits in the right conversation.
    • Check 2, completeness: I check whether the reply needs more, based on what the email says, and edit it before I send it or delete it.

Q1. Where does my agent sit on the map?

(a) Quadrant

Agents run it, a person judges (top right).

(b) The two questions

Right answer? No single right answer. Each reply depends on what the sender asks and on my commitments at the time, and a good reply could be worded many ways; only the schedule link is fixed, and I keep the schedule behind it up to date. An extra test shows this: asked to meet at the F1 2025 race (18 Oct 12:14), a good reply needed judgement, noticing the date had passed, declining politely and inviting a new date. The AI’s reply was polite but missed it, and no single rule in the prompt captured what the right reply should be.

Together? Yes. The agent runs the process on its own: it picks up each request, writes the reply and files the draft in the sender’s conversation. But I judge every draft before it leaves, because only I know my real commitments and the reply goes out in my name. My runs show why the judgement is needed: in my F1 test (see the last screenshot), the draft missed that the date had already passed, and I caught it at the draft.

(c) The person in the loop

I stand at the gate in Gmail Drafts, before anything is sent. For each draft I check the name, that no date or time is invented or already past, that the link opens, and that the draft is in the right conversation. I also catch requests that don’t make sense, such as meeting at the 2025 F1 race, which has already passed, and I add anything the email asks for that the reply misses. On its own, the agent may only read emails with “appointment” in the subject, write the reply wording, and save a draft. It has no send action, so it cannot send.

It would move to AI alone, with checks (bottom left) if the AI could read my always-up-to-date calendar, recognise scheduling emails without the word “appointment”, and spot errors such as a past date. My calendar would then decide the right reply, and the AI could work alone while I spot-check instead of judging every draft.

It would move to A person decides, AI alongside (bottom right) if I were extremely busy, my schedule had almost no free slots, and I had no booking link to share. Every request would then have to be weighed against my current commitments in my own head, so I would decide each reply myself, with the AI only helping with wording.

(d) My group’s view

I agree with three of our five placements; for two, I would answer one question differently.

Finance: analyst research (question 1, is there a right answer?). We placed “AI checks news headlines and returns key market activities and events” in AI alone, with checks, because it has a right answer.

I answer question 1 differently. Our placement names a task but no goal, and the answer depends on the goal. If the goal is to decide whether to buy or sell, there is no single right answer, because two analysts may have different risk appetites and can read the same news and decide differently. In that case the agent still gathers the news at 10 a.m., but the analyst screens which items matter for their risk, and then decides. The analyst could do this work alone, with the AI only speeding up the gathering, so my answer to question 2 stays the same as the group’s. This moves it to A person decides, AI alongside.

Operations: escalations of disputes (question 2, together or alone?). We placed this in Agents run it, a person judges. We didn’t define what kind of dispute it is. Assume a Grab passenger complains about a driver’s behaviour, e.g. taking a longer route without listening to the passenger’s advice. The passenger also uploads pictures and videos as evidence.

In this situation, I agree there is no single right answer. I would answer question 2 differently. Our placement says the AI only pulls the case information together. However, the ops person needs to examine the uploaded evidence, read the complaint, call the driver, and then decide which internal policies pulled by the AI apply to this dispute. The person still runs the dispute and makes the decision, with the AI alongside. The ops person could do this work alone, with the AI only speeding up the gathering, so I answer question 2 as “either can do alone”. This moves it to A person decides, AI alongside.

The booking link and signature are fixed text added by the draft step, so the AI cannot change the link. I also used Claude to plan the Zap, debug the Thread ID and the HTML email body, and help draft this page; I checked and edited what it gave me.
  • What I checked, and how: (1) An email without “appointment” in the subject started no run, as expected (about 2 min). (2) A request to meet at the F1 2025 race: the draft did not promise the date but did not flag it as past; rewording the prompt made little difference on the cheapest model, so I catch this at the draft (about 30 min). (3) Tricky case: a draft landed in an old conversation because the Thread field held a fixed number; I mapped it to the trigger’s Thread Id, published, and the 12:14 draft landed correctly (about 1 hour).
  • One AI error I caught: the AI did not notice that the requested date (F1 2025) had already passed.
  • Time and cost: about 1 hour to build, about 1.5 hours checking and fixing the Zap, and about 1.5 hours on documentation. $0, no card; each run uses 2 tasks (AI step and draft step). I finalised this card on 17 Oct.

Reflection

The agent has made a repetitive part of my scheduling easier: each appointment request gets a polite, personalised draft with my booking link in its own conversation, so I only review and send. It failed where the cheapest AI has no tools or judgement: it cannot see my calendar, so I had to share a Google schedule link instead; it did not notice that F1 2025 had passed; and until I mapped the Thread ID, a draft landed in the wrong conversation. On a higher tier, I would give the AI access to my calendar, so it can spot scheduling emails even without the word “appointment”, flag impossible dates and offer real free slots.
